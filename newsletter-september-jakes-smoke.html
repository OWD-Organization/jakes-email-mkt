Closing the Windows Isn’t Enough

September 2026 · Littleton & the Foothills

🌪️ SEPTEMBER 2026 · AIR QUALITY

If you’ve been outside in the last few weeks, you already know.

The sky over the Foothills has that color. The air smells like something burning hundreds of miles away — because it is.

More than 115 million Americans are under unhealthy air right now. Over 800 wildfires are burning in Canada, and the smoke that sat over the Midwest and the Northeast all month is heading south and west — Colorado could be next.

So you do what everyone says to do. Close the windows. Stay inside. Wait it out.

Here’s what nobody tells you about that.

🪟 Closing the windows isn’t enough

Researchers tracked indoor and outdoor air in more than 1,400 buildings through a smoke season. On smoke days, people sealed up — and about half as much outside air got in.

3×

Indoor particle levels still nearly tripled — in homes with the windows shut.

These were people doing everything right. Windows shut, doors closed, staying in. The air inside got three times worse anyway, because smoke particles are small enough to come through every gap a house has. Around door frames. Under the garage door. Through the HVAC. You can't seal a house against something that small.

And here's the part that matters for the rest of the year: once it's in, it doesn't leave on its own. It settles — into the carpet, the couch, the bathroom fan housing, and the ductwork your furnace is about to start blowing through every room.

Exhaust fans and returns are where smoke season shows up first — a gray film on the grille is the visible part. The duct behind it is holding the rest.

🧬 The particles are the problem

Wildfire smoke particles are about 30 times thinner than a human hair. That's what “PM2.5” means, and it's why it's in every air quality alert you've seen this month.

Particles that small don't stop in your nose or throat. They go deep into the lungs, and the smallest ones cross into the bloodstream. That's the link to asthma attacks and heart problems during smoke events — and it's why kids, who breathe more air per pound of body weight than adults, feel it first.

This isn't a bad-smell problem.

✅ What actually reduces it

Filtration. That's the intervention that shows up in the research over and over. The same studies that found tripled indoor particles found that filtering the air cuts them substantially. Here's how that translates into a real house in Littleton — during the smoke, and after it clears.

1
 Upgrade the furnace filter and run the fan — on the right filter. Your HVAC is already the biggest air filter in the house, if the filter is doing its job. Our recommendation: a pleated MERV 8, with the thermostat fan set to “On” or “Circulate” all the time, not just on smoke days. A higher MERV rating sounds better, but it restricts airflow and creates static pressure that can lock up the system and cause it to freeze — MERV 8 is the balance of airflow and filtration your system can actually handle year-round. If you really want more filtration during an active smoke week, step up to a MERV 11–13 for that week only, then go back to MERV 8. Either way, change it more often than usual this season; smoke loads a filter fast.
2
 Put a HEPA unit where people sleep. If you own a portable HEPA purifier, the bedrooms are where it earns its keep — eight hours of breathing in a closed room. Run it on high with the door shut. If you don't own one, a box fan with a MERV 13 filter taped to the back is the honest budget version, and it works.
3
 Skip the candles and the “fresh air” spray. Anything that burns or aerosolizes adds particles and VOCs to a room you're trying to clear. Same goes for running the gas stove without the hood on. During a smoke event, less is more.
4
 When it clears: get what settled out of the ducts. A filter catches what's moving. It does nothing about what already landed inside the supply and return lines during the last three smoke events — and the first cold morning you turn on the heat, the furnace pushes that back out through every register. We inspect, clean every run, hand-detail the covers, and fog the system with a plant-based sanitizer — every visit, no exceptions — to knock down the smoke odor that lives in the ductwork. Dryer vent and bathroom fans in the same visit.
5
 Then the carpet and the couch. Soft surfaces are the other filter nobody changes. Carpet and upholstery hold fine particles and smoke residue far below what a vacuum reaches, and every footstep and every flop on the cushions kicks a little back into the air — at exactly the height the kids breathe. Truck-mounted hot water extraction pulls it out; our solutions are 100% plant-based, so nobody has to stay off the floor afterward. Ducts first, carpet second, so the ducts don't re-dust a clean floor.

One to two homes a day, no rushing. Before-and-after photos of every duct and every room, so you see what came out.
